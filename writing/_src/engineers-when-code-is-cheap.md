I've become fairly convinced that the cost of writing code is heading towards pretty much zero. Probably within the next year, and for some people it's already there. I don't say that to be dramatic. It's what I see every week, depending on which hat I'm wearing.

## Two different worlds

Outside of work I spend a lot of time on open source and my own pet projects. There, things have changed completely. I work with engineers who know what good code looks like and use AI tools to move many times faster than they used to. Firmware releases that took half a year are going out in weeks, with more and better features than before. New projects spring up that are being used by hundreds of thousands of people the following week. Most of my own side projects simply wouldn't exist without these tools, because I'd never have found the time to write them by hand.

In large organisations it feels quite different, because the way we work was designed for a world where code was expensive. The process stays roughly the same, AI gets added on top, and it's easy to feel like that's enough. I don't think it is.

## Software is already being built by everyone

Something I've noticed recently is how much software is being built by individuals now, including people on my own team who aren't engineers. Small tools, dashboards, scripts, things that make their own job easier.

It's tempting to dismiss this. It's a pet project, it was built by someone non-technical, so it doesn't really count. I think that's a mistake. The models are getting better quickly, so the code is going to get better, and so will its reliability. The people building these things are also picking up coding principles from the tools as they go, in a way they never would have from a textbook. What looks like a toy today is probably an early version of how a lot of software gets made.

I've done this myself without really meaning to. Over time I've ended up building a harness for my day-to-day work that fits exactly how I think, and I know someone else who has done the same. Both are polished desktop applications. They're reliable, easy to update and we don't see crashes. So does the quality of the code underneath really matter?

In our case we both know what good looks like, so we can tell the code is pretty good, and it got there with very little steering from us. It's also getting better fast. Models like Fable 5.2, and even Opus 5.5, have really changed how much oversight they need.

## Demand isn't going away

The obvious worry with "code costs nothing" is that engineers are out of a job. I think that's wrong, and very naive.

Every time software has got cheaper to make, people have wanted a lot more of it. The pet projects above are part of that. So is every small business running on spreadsheets because proper software was never affordable, and every internal tool nobody ever had capacity to build. The demand is going up, not down. What changes is what the human part of the job looks like.

## What changes for engineers

For me the core skill becomes building the right thing at the right time. If machines are doing most of the implementation, what's left is:

- **Knowing what to build.** Understanding the customer and the problem well enough to identify what should exist.
- **Specifying it well.** Describing clearly what you want, and breaking work down so it can be built and checked, to produce quality products.
- **Knowing it's right.** Understanding what good looks like, measuring it, and evaluating what is actually good for the customer. "Taste" is a word being bandied around a lot more now, and it tries to capture this.

None of this is new. They're the skills we've always wanted from engineers and product people, but they were hard to hire for and implementation ability trumped them. We always talk about them, though: ownership, curiosity, customer focus. These are the hard skills people now need. Previously, plenty of good careers were built on turning someone else's vision into working code.

I think that's the part that changes. Being product focused and having taste stops being a nice extra for the most senior people and becomes part of every engineer's job.

## Invest in people, not just tools

It's tempting to look for tooling to fix this. What I think we need to do is give engineers the time and space to grow those skills: writing specs, and being asked to solve customer needs rather than implement someone else's idea. On the ground, that could mean less ambiguous tickets, a much closer working relationship with product people and learning from them, and working closely with the non-engineers building their own tools, who are probably further along in some ways than we give them credit for.

It won't change overnight, and I'm sure bigger organisations will generally move slower than others, but the sooner we start, the easier it'll be. I know this is difficult to talk about, and the me from three years ago would be laughing in my face, but having seen this from both sides I'm fairly sure it's coming. I don't think companies still producing software in more traditional ways will be able to compete on speed to market, adaptability, reacting to market trends and, over time, quality. Yes, quality.
