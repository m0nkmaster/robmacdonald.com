Last night (15th May 2025), I had the honour of being part of a panel discussion in Manchester on the evolving role of Product Engineering - what it actually means and why it’s become so important in today’s rapidly changing software industry. It was a fantastic event: thought-provoking discussions, great audience, and superb organisation. I genuinely loved being part of it.

Ahead of the event, I spent some time jotting down my thoughts on the key topics we might cover. Now that it’s over, I thought I’d share those notes here. If you weren’t able to attend, I hope this gives you a sense of the discussions we had and the points we explored.

#### How do you define a product engineer, and why is it gaining traction now?

My view is that a Product Engineer is a kind of "heads-up programmer." A programmer heavily focused on the customer and the product. Unlike engineers focused on the technical depth of their role, product engineers bias for outcomes - creating value for customers and achieving business goals through tech.

Product Engineers are software engineers at their core, but they bring a business-oriented mindset. They prioritise the customer's needs, constantly asking, "Why are we doing this?", "Is this the best approach?", or "Can we deliver greater value in a simpler, faster, or cheaper way?" Their focus isn't on the most technically complex or ambitious solution but on what delivers the best results.

Consider them as problem solvers who like to step back, evaluate options critically, and advocate for practical solutions that balance quality, efficiency, and customer value. This "heads-up" approach is key and allows them to bridge engineering with product thinking effectively.

#### How does this compare to past movements, and what lessons can we learn?

In some ways, the idea of product engineering feels like a return to earlier software development days. When I started back in 1999 🥹, roles were less specialised. I was a "website designer," which meant wearing many hats: using Photoshop, writing code in text editors, hosting websites, managing DNS, SEO, speaking directly with customers, database management, scaling architectures, writing proposals, and considering usability and accessibility.

There wasn’t so much the clear separation of roles as there is now. Instead, you had to span disciplines from UI and UX design to scalable system architectures. Books like [Don’t Make Me Think](https://sensible.com/dont-make-me-think/) by Steve Krug on one hand, and [The Pragmatic Programmer](https://pragprog.com/titles/tpp20/the-pragmatic-programmer-20th-anniversary-edition/) by David Thomas and Andrew Hunt on the other were equally well-read books on my shelf.

The close link to the customer and ability to influence every aspect of a product made the process incredibly fulfilling and rapid. However, the tech industry has now become deeply specialised. With the rise of cloud engineering, front-end, back-end, and new tooling and libraries have widened these knowledge niches, we now often rely on multiple teams/people to do what one team with less defined roles did before.

This has benefits, but we have lost something and introduced step-changes in the overall performance and quality of software. We’ve introduced more organisational dependencies across teams. Dependencies always slow down delivery and a whole fields exists to attempt to resolve these. Books like [Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/) by Matt Skelton and Manuel Pais is a great place to start on this topic.

I've noticed a shift over the past ten years, I think of it as subtle backlash against this hyper-specialisation. The "full-stack engineer" is what springs to mind. Engineers taking a broader responsibility for the product; a more holistic approach reminiscent of those earlier rose-tinted days. While we haven't returned to a world where single engineers cover everything from Photoshop designs to database designs, the idea of a team owning a wider scope of responsibilities is still appealing. Cross-functional teams essentially represent this.

To me, product engineering represents a modern iteration of this movement. It’s about combining technical skills with customer obsession, which is a massive focus at Amazon. When I was at Amazon, I found that everything really does revolve around the customer. One of their core principles is “[working backward](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Working-Backwards-Insights-Stories-Secrets/dp/1529033829)”: starting from the customer’s needs and then figuring out how to build it. That’s the mindset of a product engineer. It’s not about creating the most intricate architecture, or even the very best technical solution, it’s about building valuable, and impactful products first and foremost.

#### How could Product Engineering reshape team structures, collaboration, and decision-making?

Product engineering will absolutely reshape team structures and how we collaborate. It shifts the focus from rigid roles to a more fluid, outcome-driven approach.

Teams may become smaller and more agile, with product engineers taking on broader responsibilities. Organisations may no longer need extensive layers of specialised teams and roles. Instead, you need people who are curious, eager to learn, and willing to try new things. In some cases, a smaller team of collaborative people can achieve more. Fewer dependencies, less legacy - both a major cause of project delays.

Second, product engineering fosters a shared understanding across the team. By minimising distinctions between roles, people naturally gain a broader view of the project; supercharging collaboration. The ability to fact-check each other and learn from each other becomes a superpower, creating an environment where decision-making is collective, informed and reduces silos of knowledge. Lessening the likelihood of suffering the ‘Brent problem’ like in the [Phoenix Project](https://www.amazon.co.uk/Phoenix-Project-DevOps-Helping-Business/dp/0988262592).

This also adds variety to work, a crucial factor in job satisfaction. When people can take on different tasks instead of sticking to a narrow specialisation, it can reignite excitement and engagement within a team.

A product engineering approach enables teams to operate like startups within larger organisations. It encourages a fluid, "all hands on deck" mindset, where team members ask, "Who can help with this?" or "Who wants to take on this challenge?" Teams prioritise outcomes over titles. Just focusing on what it takes to get things built, tested, and shipped for the customer.

#### What can leaders do to support teams making this shift?

I think it's super important to ensure that everyone understands what's expected of them. If companies want to adopt a more product engineering approach, they need to bake that into appraisals, reviews, and job descriptions. It absolutely isn't for everyone so if this is the approach you want to take, you need to make sure the people want this kind of role.

Ensure everyone knows the purpose - OKRs and other cascading, visible and collaborative objective setting mechanisms can help bring everyone together around the company’s vision.

We’re asking people to be more strategic and curious about how their software will be used. Asking questions like “Why are we building this?”, “Have you thought about this other option?” - rather than primarily providing technical expertise and letting a, somewhat removed, product person spoon feed requirements, user stories and Jira tickets. It’s about integrating this into job roles and expectations, not just hoping for a bit more customer-focus from your engineers. Hoping they don’t want to code all the time.

I foresee difficult conversations in some situations. I don't have all the answers.

#### What impact is AI having on software engineering, and how will it evolve?

It is already massive and is only going to get bigger. AI is transforming software engineering already by lowering the barrier to entry and speeding up development. It means a broader range of technical people can contribute. It’s not about everyone suddenly being able to create amazing, good quality software; more about empowering experienced engineers, even those no longer coding daily (like me!), to contribute. Beware the Engineering Managers and Principal Engineers jumping back into coding! 😬

Let me talk about my experience for a moment; I’ve been an engineer for over twelve years, followed by a manager for eight. While I’m no longer hands-on at work, I like to think I’ve learnt enough (and continue to be curious enough) that I know what good software looks like. That’s critical - I can’t state it enough. Years of hands-on experience and working with and learning from awesome people have given me a strong foundation in best practices - clean code, object-oriented design, functional design, scalable architectures, TDD, coding paradigms, handling technical debt, deployment pipelines, but most importantly how to learn. This grounding allows me to evaluate and review code or architectures proposed by generative AI tools with some confidence.

AI tools have enabled me to get more hands-on again, allowing me to quickly prototype solutions, hack scripts, contribute to open source more and get more time on pet projects.

To give you a concrete example, I recently contributed to an open-source project building the firmware for a music synth and drum machine in C++, a language I’d never touched before. Assisted by AI, I was able to ramp up quickly, understand the codebase, the idiosyncrasies of the language, and produce well-received quality code that achieved my aims. I contributed because I am an actual user of the product I love and I saw a missing feature. This is an example of literally starting from the customer and working backwards - made possible through AI.

But again, knowing what good looks like is the key to using tools like Cursor, Windsurf and GitHub Copilot with confidence. If you can’t confidently review, course correct, or refine AI-generated code, it’s very dangerous and will result in unmaintainable solutions.

For experienced developers though, AI bridges that gap between deep technical specific know-how and product-focused thinking. It allows people like me to re-engage in coding or code reviews, even in unfamiliar languages. At the same time, it works the other way; it frees hands-on engineers by reducing the time needed to be spent on the nitty-gritty detail. Things like remembering the exact syntax, function names, debugging frustrating errors, searching Stack Overflow for hours on end. Refactoring becomes a job of hours not days or even weeks. This enables engineers to focus more time on higher-level problems, such as architecture, problem-solving, and understanding the customer and product. In a recent [interview, Michael Truell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=En5cSXgGvZM) (CEO of Cursor) captured this perfectly describing how engineers will focus more on their “Taste” of the software they are building and less on the fine detail - day to day software engineering is going to become more about “having the right idea” and less about implementation detail.

I feel this just continues a long trend in software development - a move to more declarative engineering. Just as most of us are way beyond working with Assembly and languages which require memory management, we focus on higher-order concerns like readability and maintainability, scaling, resilience, testability. AI is actually making “human language engineering” a reality.

Over the years, we’ve seen a lot of tools emerge to make software development faster and easier like Kubernetes, Lambda, cloud services, frameworks like React, and countless others... These have removed many of the concerns we used to deal with - whilst introducing a lot of new ones imho 😉). I think AI is the same; the next step in this - a new layer of abstraction from what’s actually happening in the silicon.

#### What organisational and leadership shifts are needed to make product engineering work?

The criteria we focus on when hiring. I think we should approach recruiting more holistically, prioritising behaviours such as having strong ownership, being outcome focused, having deep curiosity over specific language experience. People who love problem solving ‘The Generalist’ as described, far better than a could, in a great article in Every recently by Dan Shipper; [Why Generalists Own the Future](https://every.to/chain-of-thought/why-generalists-own-the-future).

By generalists, I mean individuals who are naturally curious, eager to explore diverse domains, and excel at problem solving. They may not have the deepest knowledge in one area, a specific language for example, but they thrive on gaining breadth of knowledge and asking many questions. This aligns with a core leadership principle used at Amazon a lot: “[Learn and Be Curious](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles)”.

With AI tools maturing rapidly, generalists are becoming more empowered than ever to quickly up-skill, tackle new languages or projects, and focus on solving problems creatively without having to spend the time mastering the domain.

#### What are the risks? Both for engineers adapting to this shift and for teams adopting product engineering and AI.

Product engineers are expected to wear multiple hats - have strong technical grounding with customer-focused thinking. However, not every engineer is eager to focus less on their code, their craft. We are asking a lot. This will lead to anxiety and frustration. As we talk about smaller cross-functional teams with minimal dependencies, it places pressure on individuals to deliver across a wide skill set. However, as I think is clear, I truly believe AI will be a game changer on this front.

Another risk of AI tools is their increasing reliance on services owned by a handful of large companies, which are expensive to operate. We're moving beyond standalone IDEs on our own devices to tools that depend on external data centres and companies. This reliance introduces vulnerabilities. What if access is interrupted? Over-dependence on AI tools could leave us unprepared to maintain or develop systems independently. We’re putting a lot of trust in the hands of a few tech giants.

There's also a risk of skill erosion. AI will enable us to write great software, but are we losing the "muscle" of understanding how to do it without those tools? If engineers no longer practice the fundamentals, how specific languages work, or foundational development concepts, work could grind to a halt when AI tools fail or aren't available. It’s similar to the pre-internet boom days: how did we manage before we could Google bugs or check Stack Overflow? But reliance on AI tools will magnify this challenge.

Another factor is cost. As AI tools evolve, providers like OpenAI and Anthropic are refining their subscription models, and costs are climbing. What was free or low-cost a year ago is growing expensive - £30, £40, £50 per month for developers who rely on AI daily. These increases could make advanced tools inaccessible for some teams. It’s a little like the recent episode of [Black Mirror (Common People)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_People_(Black_Mirror)) where a revolutionary medical subscription service allows people to continue living after a major brain injury… for an ever increasing monthly fee.

For inexperienced engineers, the risks are even higher. Early in their careers, they may lean too heavily on AI, skipping the crucial grounding in “what good software looks like.” Clean code principles, architectures, and strong design patterns span languages and require practice. Using AI to bypass this foundational learning risks leaving engineers with shallow expertise. My advice? Focus on writing software by hand, understand the architecture, and build those skills early before you begin to use AI more heavily.

#### Where is software engineering heading, and how can teams prepare for the future?

I don’t think software engineers are going anywhere. But I do think we’ll need fewer people to achieve the same amount of output. However, the market will grow as building solutions become cheaper. The role of a software engineer will evolve. Engineers will need to become AI “super-users.” The essential skills will be in working with AI effectively, understanding how to guide tools effectively, mastering prompts, data integrations. We’re entering a world where it is more important to understand What to build, less about the How.

What won’t change is the need for curious, learning-focused, and technically-minded problem solvers. But the nature of our work is evolving. Engineers will replace much of the hands-on coding for designing architectures, solving high-level challenges, and understanding customer needs. The demand for technical expertise remains, but the future belongs to those who can adapt, innovate, and master new skills. Many of these skills which probably don't even exist. Yet.
